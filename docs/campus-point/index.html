Spring Seasonal Change at Campus Point — UCSB Lagoon Field Study

field study
ecology

ENVS 153
An 8-week field observation study tracking seasonal changes in plants, pollinators, and bird species richness at UCSB’s Campus Point lagoon.
Published

May 28, 2026

Overview

As part of ENVS 153 at UC Santa Barbara, our team conducted an 8-week field study tracking spring seasonal changes in vegetation, pollinators, and bird diversity at Campus Point — a coastal sage scrub and grassland site located next to Campus Point Beach, adjacent to UCSB’s Cheadle Center restoration area.

Team: Izzy Ribondo, Yaasiel Leos, and Zhelin Qi

Location: UCSB Campus Lagoon, Goleta, CA (34°24’22.7”N, 119°50’39.8”W)

Why Campus Point?

We chose this site because of its rich ecological diversity and its position at the edge of the lagoon ecosystem. It offered:

  • High abundance of Wild Radish (Raphanus sativus) and Great Brome (Bromus diandrus) for tracking non-native plant changes
  • Presence of birds, pollinators, and lagoon wildlife
  • Proximity to the Cheadle Center coastal restoration area
  • A mix of native and non-native species expected to show rapid seasonal change through spring

A native Torrey Pine (Pinus torreyana) — one of North America’s rarest pine species — was also present at the site, supporting lichen growth and hummingbird nesting.

Research Questions

We investigated three main questions:

Q1: How does native and non-native vegetation change throughout the season near the lagoon at UCSB?

Q2: How does the increase in flowering plants affect pollinator abundance and activity near Campus Point?

Q3: How did bird species richness change throughout spring at Campus Point?

Methods

Every Thursday at 1:00 PM over 8 weeks, we visited the same site and recorded:

  • Temperature, weather, and site conditions
  • Bird species (identified with Merlin Bird ID)
  • Plant species and flower/seed counts (identified with Seek and iNaturalist)
  • 5-minute pollinator surveys
  • Photos and timelapse documentation of site change

Results

Bird Species Richness

Bird species richness increased from 2 species in Week 1 to 10 species by Week 8, with a strong positive trend (R² = 0.8). Seasonal vegetation growth and increased flower availability likely supported greater bird diversity over time.

Plant Flower and Seed Counts

Wild Radish dominated early in the season, peaking in Week 4 with over 400 flower/seed heads, before declining sharply. Great Brome increased gradually through mid-season. Invasive species abundance consistently exceeded native species counts, which matched our prediction that non-native annuals would dominate as the season progressed.

Pollinator Abundance

Pollinator counts followed the flowering pattern closely — abundance increased as Wild Radish bloomed, peaked in Week 7 at 13 pollinators per 5 minutes, then declined in Week 8 as flowers senesced. Pollinators observed included Western Honeybees, cabbage white butterflies, and hummingbirds.

Key Takeaways

This project gave me a firsthand look at how seasonal change drives cascading ecological effects — from plant phenology to insect activity to bird diversity. Watching the site transform week by week, from lush green vegetation in early spring to dry, seed-heavy grasses by late May, made abstract ecological concepts tangible. It also reinforced the impact of invasive species: Wild Radish and Great Brome consistently dominated the site, shaping the resources available to every other organism present.
